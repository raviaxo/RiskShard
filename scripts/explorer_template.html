RISKSHARD

Public cyber-loss evidence
and what each figure can and cannot support

Every figure traces to a named public source, states the quantity it actually measures, names the population it was measured on, and declares what it cannot bear. The loss simulation further down each item is a reference rendering of that evidence — not the product.

This page is generated from the open repository — nothing on it is hand-written. Each item covers one jurisdiction / industry / threat cell and lists the public evidence found for it: every parameter states its source, the exact line cited, the quantity it measures, the population it was measured on, and its limitation. Any line can be disputed as a GitHub issue.

The evidence is the exhibit; the simulation at the foot of each item is a reference rendering of it. It is shown because composing these anchors is what exposes their defects — every declaration on this page exists because there was a distribution to inspect — and not because the output is the thing being offered. Whether this evidence belongs in your model, and in what shape, is your decision to make.

Read the maturity honestly: these are governed-starter and review-candidate shards, not human-approved benchmarks. Loss tails (P95/P99) are directional, not precise.

Compute this against your own cell

Every fit line below is computed against our cell, which is a convenience for one reader — us. Name yours and every one of them recomputes, by the same rule, in your browser. Nothing is sent anywhere and nothing is stored.

This tells you what is far from you, not what is good for you. A geography mismatch is fatal to one analysis and irrelevant to the next, and only you know which — so this page publishes no fit score, no grade and no ranking, and it will not reorder anything to suggest one. Matched means the declared population names every facet you set. It does not mean the figure belongs in your model. Leave a facet unset and it is simply not tested. The status column and the counts at the top of this page continue to describe our cell — those are the released figures, cited in the data pack; yours change only what you see.

Notice to readers — how to break a number
  1. Pick any figure in any item below.
  2. Follow it to the named source and the exact cited line — both are printed on the parameter's row.
  3. If the value, the source, or the caveat is wrong, use the [dispute] link on that row. It opens a pre-filled issue; state what is wrong and the public source that shows it.

Most readers won't break one — the easier ways in count just as much: request the cell you need (requests decide what gets built next) · name a closer source (naming it is a full contribution — extraction is on us) · argue with an open judgment call (no source-hunting required).

We have already broken several of our own: what governing this evidence turned up — including two figures retracted for citing no primary source and two claims withdrawn after measurement contradicted them.

Note 1 — Basis of preparation

Monte Carlo, 10,000 trials, seed 42, machine-independent seeding — a modeled range from public evidence, not a prediction. A parameter marked estimate is an interpretive value whose limitation is stated on its face; all other parameters cite a named public source. A parameter marked bridged is source-backed by evidence not drawn from this item's own cell — the dimensions borrowed across (country, sector, size, threat) are named on its fit line, and that chip is what the cell-matched / bridged counts at the top of this page tally. Bridging is declared, never hidden: a reader counting the source-backed total should not infer more cell-specific evidence than exists. Grades follow the maturity ladder: governed_starter → benchmark_review_candidate → benchmark-grade (the last only after a recorded human review; no item on this page holds it).

Fit is relative to a target, and the target on this page is ours. Each parameter prints two lines and the order is deliberate. declared for is the population the source measured — countries, industries, size bands, threats. That is a property of the record and it is true for every reader. fit is computed from that population against this item's own cell, which the line names; it tells you how the record sits relative to ours and almost nothing about yours. It is stored nowhere. If your cell differs, and it will, that is the one line to recompute, and the line above it does not move.

That the first line means what it says is a repair, not an assumption. On 2026-08-14, 21 records were found declaring the cell they were borrowed for rather than the population measured — the US BEC frequency floor declaring financial-services mid-market over an economy-wide numerator and denominator, three US data-breach frequencies declaring US over a UK survey, two Singapore anchors declaring SG over US data. All 21 were corrected, no published figure moved, and a test now fails the build if another appears. Every fit line below is therefore earned: the population above it genuinely does not name this cell.

The cell-matched count above got much worse on 2026-08-15, and that is the correction rather than a decline. Fit used to be a second field an author maintained by hand beside the declaration. Measured against those declarations it disagreed on 45 of 66 parameters, counting the same all-industry declaration as borrowed 28 times and as matched 72. It was retired: fit is now computed on one rule for every facet — bridged when the declared population does not name this cell's value — so cell-matched fell 31 → 7 and bridged rose 35 → 59. No published figure moved; every value, source, caveat and simulated total is unchanged. 7 is what the number always was. One consequence is worth knowing while reading: a statutory penalty cap, a documented single-event loss and a same-survey adjacent band are all measured over some other population, so they now read bridged too. The measures line is what tells you which of those a parameter is.

This page publishes no fit score, grade or percentage, and will not. Compressing these facets into one number would assert that a geography mismatch and a size mismatch trade off against each other in a way we cannot know for your scenario — a geography mismatch is fatal to one analysis and irrelevant to the next. The facets are listed separately so you decide which ones bite.

Each parameter also states what quantity it measures on its measures line. This is independent of the source and of the population: two anchors can both be drawn from this item's own cell and still measure different things — an annual breach prevalence against an event frequency, or a perceived cost against an average total loss against a regulatory penalty. Where a range's minimum, likely and maximum do not share one basis it is marked a mixed range above that item's table, with the differing bases named. A mixed range is not an error and nothing here is hidden because of it — each figure remains separately sourced — but the width between the anchors is then partly a difference of definition rather than of uncertainty, and a reader should not treat it as a spread. This declaration was added after a practitioner asked the question in the open; which mixes are acceptable is not yet settled and is recorded as an open question in the repository, not decided quietly.

A maximum on this page is not a bound. Each item's impact.max also states what is known about it being exceeded, on its exceedance line. Most say none known, and that is the honest answer rather than an omission: published loss distributions carrying tail quantiles are rare and mostly commercial, so nearly every public maximum is one documented event, the largest row in someone's dataset, or a legal ceiling. Read those as the largest loss found, never the largest loss that can happen — a figure with no exceedance statement says a loss that size occurred, not how often a loss is worse. This matters more than it looks: because the model composes minimum, likely and maximum into one distribution, the maximum drives the modeled average rather than capping it, so it is simultaneously the least evidenced anchor and the one the result is most sensitive to. Where an exceedance can be stated the line gives it — and the rate shown is a within-sample rate on insured claims, which is a floor: losses above it are more common than the sample says, not less.

How much of each figure comes from that maximum is stated on its face. The model composes minimum, likely and maximum into one distribution whose average is a weighted blend of the three, so the maximum's share of the modeled average can be computed exactly — no sampling, no seed, no error term. Where that share exceeds half, the item says so directly under its loss figure. Read it together with that item's exceedance line: a figure mostly produced by an anchor that admits no exceedance probability is a figure resting on one observation, however carefully that observation was sourced. Run python scripts/riskshard_modules.py tail for the full table, including what each item's annual average does when only the maximum is moved.
