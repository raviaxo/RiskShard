Japan's National Police Agency, which collects every ransomware case reported
to Japanese police, published its 2025 numbers. The report has three
denominators.

226 organizations reported ransomware damage.
89 of them answered the investigation-cost question.
107 of them answered the recovery-duration question.

Take "10 to 50 million yen is the most common cyber cost in Japan" into a
board paper and you have misrepresented two things. That band is 32 of 89
cases, so you have made it sound almost three times more common than the
report supports. And it is the cost of investigating the incident, not the
cost of the incident. You have shown your board a forensics invoice and
called it the loss.

A denominator is what the number was divided by, and it decides what the
number means.

"48% of payments were over a million dollars" means 48% of the companies
that paid a ransom. If 100 companies are attacked and 20 of them pay, that
sentence describes about 10 companies. Put it in front of your board as an
attack statistic and it describes 48. Same sentence, five times the
exposure, and nobody in the room can see which one you meant.

Most publishers are not hiding this. Sophos states that its ransomware
figures cover organizations that were hit rather than all organizations,
and states that it removed ransoms of 40 million dollars and above before
publishing. The National Police Agency labels which table holds which base.
The qualification is in the report. It is not in the sentence you copy out
of the report, and the sentence is what travels.

So before a number goes in front of your board, find what it was divided
by. If you cannot find it in under five minutes, the report has not earned
a place in that paper.

What I am working on from here is making that findable without reading all
65 reports yourself. For every headline figure, what it was divided by, and
whether the publisher stated it or you have to work it out.
