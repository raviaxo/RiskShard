Eighteen years in governance and compliance, and the moment I still see go
wrong is the same one every time. Someone puts a cyber-loss number in front
of a board, the board asks where it came from, and the honest answer is a
vendor report nobody in the room has read.

So I built the thing I wanted to have in that meeting.

RiskShard is an open dataset of cyber-loss figures where every number
carries the source it came from, the exact line in that source, and what
the number cannot be used for. Eleven cells across eight countries.
Sixty-six parameters, every one traced to a public primary source, none of
them mine.

Three things you can do with it today.

Look up a cell. Country, industry, size, threat. You get a dollar range you
can defend, with every input showing its source underneath it rather than
in a footnote nobody opens.

Follow any figure to the line it came from. One click, into the
publisher's own document. If a number cannot survive that walk, it should
not be in your board paper, and this is how you find out before the board
does.

Read the audit. I have read 65 public cyber-loss reports against their own
archived files and recorded what each one actually publishes. Whether it
gives you a most-likely value. Whether it shows the spread. Whether it says
how often losses go bigger. Who was actually measured. Most reports answer
fewer of those than people assume.

Here is what it will not do. It will not give you a number for a cell it
has not measured. Eleven cells is a small number and I would rather publish
eleven I can defend than five hundred I cannot. When a figure gets broken,
it gets retracted in public with the correction on the record.

Everything is free, open source, and built to be argued with. If you work
with these numbers and something here is wrong, I would rather hear it from
you than find it later.

The work from here is making the qualifications travel with the numbers,
instead of getting left behind in the report.

NOTES
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Opens on the room, not the project. The board-asks-where-it-came-from
moment is the thing the audience has lived and it needs no setup.

"None of them mine" is load-bearing. It is the whole claim in three words
and it separates this from every vendor dataset.

The three capabilities are the "ability" ask. Each one is a verb the reader
can do, not a feature the project has.

The refusal paragraph is the credibility. Eleven defended beats five
hundred asserted, and saying so out loud is what makes the eleven worth
something. It also pre-empts the obvious objection rather than waiting for
it in the comments.

CTA is the journey line, first person, no instruction to the reader.
